Peak-Hour Delivery Recovery — when a lower cancellation rate isn't enough

An illustrative food-delivery scenario evaluating operational causes, customer communication and the economics of delay compensation.

Detailed case study (PDF) ↗

Frame the problem before the solution

At peak dinner hours, demand may exceed courier capacity and delay orders. Cancellation can happen at different stages—from restaurant preparation to courier assignment or pickup. Those stages imply different causes, so a single coupon policy is unlikely to solve the underlying supply constraint.

Options considered

I separated operational remedies from customer-facing recovery: more transparent late-order notifications; narrowly targeted compensation where it may change behavior; and supply-side approaches such as dispatch or batching improvements. The analysis centers on a notification-only control versus notification plus coupon, with careful attention to whom the offer reaches.

Illustrative experiment and unit economics

Suppose cancellation is 14% with notification only and 11% with a coupon, while incentives average ₹18 per eligible order. Across 100 eligible orders, that means three additional orders saved at a ₹1,800 incentive cost—or ₹600 per incremental cancellation avoided before incremental contribution or repeat-customer value. A better cancellation rate alone cannot justify rollout.

Decision and safeguards

Start with proactive, accurate delay updates. Test compensation only where lateness severity, cancellation risk and expected incremental value justify it. Monitor contribution margin, true incremental retained orders, support complaints and repeat orders—while avoiding rewarding customers who would have waited anyway.

Evidence boundary

All percentages, cost inputs and experiment results are fictional scenario assumptions for analytical practice. They are not Swiggy or Zomato data, nor findings from an actual A/B test.
